Technology

Built so losing us is survivable

This page is for the people who actually build publishing tools and for anyone who doesn't trust a platform with their work without checking underneath. It is also the reason the promises on the other pages are structural rather than sentimental.

What the architecture guarantees

Three decisions, and everything else follows

1

One uniform kind of thing

Every piece of work — poem, story, recording, gallery — is stored the same way. Nothing about the system knows what a poem is.

This is why new formats are cheap and why nothing you publish gets stranded in a format-specific corner.

2

The paywall is attached to the work

The access rule lives with the content it protects, so it cannot drift out of sync with it. Editing or unpublishing a piece takes its rules with it.

This is also why there is no way to end up with an orphaned rule pointing at deleted content.

3

One authority on access

Every read of a piece asks a single question of a single component: may this reader open this? The website, search, apps and email jobs all use that one answer.

A paywall that can be bypassed by finding the forgotten code path somewhere else isn't a paywall.

What this buys you as a writer: the promise that a locked poem stays locked is enforced in the same place the poem is stored, rather than in whichever screen happened to render it. And because the rule travels with the work, you can never accidentally publish something you meant to keep paid.

The data model

Four parts, in plain language

Here is the whole model, without jargon. If you're technical, the actual schema is linked at the bottom and it's readable.

The work itself

One record per piece. Who made it, what it's called, when it went live, whether it's public or locked, and any details specific to that format. A poem and a recording differ only in what they store as extra details.

The content

The body — the words, or the reference to the audio or images. Stored separately from the record above, because it's the part that changes when you revise something.

The parts

Chapters, stanzas, or tracks, in order, each able to carry its own lock. A poem might have one part; an album has twelve.

The files

Audio and images, stored once and referenced wherever they appear. The same cover image can head your profile, your page, and a post without being uploaded three times.

Note what isn't there: no columns for "poem", "story", or "album". No table that only makes sense for text. That's the entire trick, and it's why a new format doesn't require changing anything that already works.

Access control

How a paywall actually gets enforced

This is the part worth being precise about, because "we have paywalls" means nothing until you know where the check lives.

Every attempt to open a locked piece resolves to one decision, made in one place, returning a simple answer: full access, a partial preview, or nothing. The same decision governs the website, the API, search results, background email jobs, and any future app. There is no second implementation to forget to update.

  • The creator always wins. Authors and credited collaborators can always open their own work, including drafts.
  • Unpublished is never visible — not to followers, not to paying members, not to anybody holding a direct link. Not even by accident.
  • Paid access is additive. A subscription, a one-off purchase, and a gifted membership all satisfy the same requirement. Readers never have to wonder which route bought them access.
  • Partial access is real, not a stub. A locked story returns its permitted chapters and marks the rest, rather than returning an error.
  • Payments and member details are isolated from the public parts of the system, so a mistake in one area can't expose another.

Why paywall rules live with the content: the common failure in publishing software is a lock that keeps pointing at work you deleted, or a piece that goes live while its lock was never saved. Here, deleting the work deletes the rule with it, in the same operation.

Verified, not assumed

The schema was applied to a real database and the access rules were tested against real data. Every case below was checked by an assertion that fails loudly if the behaviour changes.

  • Anonymous reader on a free piece → full access
  • Anonymous reader on a locked piece → preview only
  • Signed in, no membership → preview only
  • Member above the tier → full access
  • Member below the tier → preview only
  • One-off purchase → full access, permanently
  • Draft, viewed by a paying member → nothing
  • 3-chapter story, 2 locked → non-member sees 1, member sees 3
  • Paging a feed → no item repeated, none skipped

Five real defects were found and fixed this way, including a paywall rule that silently hid locked work from feeds.

Under the hood

The stack, briefly

  • PostgreSQL — the work, the members, the money, and the access rules all live in one well-understood database.
  • Python or Node for the API. Undecided at this stage; the data model doesn't constrain it.
  • Object storage for audio and images, addressed independently of any page — so a file survives a redesign.
  • A payments provider handling cards, and us handling everything above and below it.

Deliberately boring

Nothing here is exotic, and nothing is a dependency you can't audit. There is no proprietary content format, no closed store of your own work, and no service that can only be read by one company's software.

Boring is the goal. This is infrastructure someone has to keep running for other people's writing, and the least interesting choices available are the ones least likely to fail at 3am.

Money and safety

Where the payments live

Kept separate

Card details never touch our systems. They go from the reader to the payment provider, so we never hold something we'd have to worry about losing.

Reconciled line by line

Gross, platform fee, and your net are recorded separately on every transaction. A mismatch should be explainable, and it should be visible.

Idempotent

Payment providers retry webhooks. We record each transaction against a unique key, so a repeated notification can never charge you twice or pay you twice.

One thing worth saying plainly: we store enough about a payment to pay you and to handle tax. We don't build behavioural profiles from it, we don't sell transaction data, and we don't use your members' payment history to decide what to show other readers.

For developers

The actual schema is readable

If you build publishing tools, you are welcome to read it and tell me what would break. That invitation is genuine; I'd rather find the problem now.

  • Plain SQL, heavily commented. No ORM layer hiding the shape, no generated migrations. You can read the whole thing in an afternoon.
  • Reproducible. One file applies cleanly to an empty database, and a second populates it with realistic data.
  • The tests are executable, not descriptive. They assert behaviour and fail loudly if a paywall rule regresses.
  • Extending it is a documented operation. Adding a format is demonstrated in the test suite itself: register it, add its optional table, and publish into it, with the core untouched.

If you're evaluating this platform, the useful question is what breaks. Ask and I'll answer concretely — including the things that are genuinely hard, which right now are real-time collaboration on a document and anything resembling a rich text editor that doesn't destroy poetry.

What I'd want to be asked

  • What happens to a creator's archive if you fold?
  • Can a reader get their data out, including purchases?
  • What stops a paywall being bypassed via the API?
  • How are failed payments handled without punishing readers?
  • What are you actually charging, and what changes that?

All five have answers, and the first two have code behind them.
